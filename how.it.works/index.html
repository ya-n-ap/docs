    How Does YANAP Work

    Default Internet Access Flow

    If you have never touched your DNS configuration in your LAN environment, then your situation is similar to the diagram below.

    Think of DNS like the internet's address book. When you do anything online, the first step is your device asking a DNS server where to go.

    Diagram 1 — Illustrative sketch of the internet access flow with default DNS   /   Drawing with Excalidraw.   /   Click the image to enlarge.


    graph LR
  A[Your PC, Phone, Smart TV/Speakers/Plugs, Camera, etc.] -->|➊ send **URL** to| B[DNS Server];
  B -->|➋ reply with matching **IP** to| A;
  A -->|➌ use **IP** to connect to| C[Content / App Servers];
    Diagram 2 — Flowchart of the internet access flow with default DNS


    "Default" — In other words, you are using the DNS picked by your ISP to serve Their Own Interests.This can lead to the following potential1 consequences:

    • They, your ISP and the DNS service provider, have your metadata — for example, when you go online, and which websites, services, or apps you use. They can't see the full content, but even without that, the metadata alone is usually enough to reconstruct your activity: whether you were watching videos, reading posts, browsing images, or playing games.
    • Often, they sell your metadata to data brokers, ad networks, or other third parties. That's when things get more invasive. Once it's combined with other data sources, they can often figure out exactly what video you watched, which product you checked out, whose profile you viewed, what you searched — all of it. With enough data, you can be identified through profiling and social engineering.
    • Even if your ISP doesn't sell your data, there's still a risk it could be leaked or misused. This could happen through a data breach, careless employees, or government access.
    • In the worst-case scenario, if the DNS server itself is doing evil things, your online safety is seriously at risk. You could be silently redirected to phishing sites, malware, or scams without even realizing it.

    These points don't cover every possible risk, but they do show some common ones.


    Internet Access Flow with DNS-Based Blocker

    The AdGuard Home I'm using in YANAP is not the only DNS-based blocker out there. There are other FOSS options that offer similar features, such as Pi-Hole, Technitium, and others. With these DNS filters or blockers, your internet access work like the diagrams 3 and 4.

    Diagram 3 — Illustrative sketch of the internet access flow with DNS-Based Blocker   /   Drawing with Excalidraw.   /   Click the image to enlarge.


    graph LR
  A[Your Devices] -->|➊ send **URL** to| B[DNS Server];
  B --> |check Blocklist| D{Blocked?};
  D --> |No ➋ send **IP** to| A;
  D --> |Yes ➋ send **0.0.0.0 - Not Found** to| A;
  A --> C{Got Real IP?};
  C -->|Yes ➌ connect to| E[Content / App Servers];
  C -->|No ➌ drop into| F[BlackHole];
    Diagram 4 — Flowchart of the internet access flow with DNS-Based Blocker

    In this way, you regain all some control over your internet traffic.


    Can & Can't

    A DNS-based blocker isn't a silver bullet, but it's definitely a solid start.

    • Can Block

      # most _____ on websites and apps

      • ads, affiliate
      • tracker, bug tracker, telemetry, metrics
      • Malware, Cryptojacking, scam, spam, phishing
      • social media widgets, in-page pop-ups, other annoyances
      • fake stores, fake streaming, rip-offs
      • native broadband tracker
      • badware hoster
      • malicious scripts
      • adult content
      • gambling content

      # any website you want

      # an entire online service2

    • Can't Block

      # _____ disguised as normal content3

      • video ads on Youtube, Instagram, etc.
      • sponsored posts and ads inside Facebook, TikTok, etc.

      # _____ that doesn’t access internet

      • built-in offline app ads
      • intranet-initiated attacks

      # _____ that doesn’t use your DNS

      • content with fixed IPs
      • content with fixed DNS

      # in-page or in-app scripts, like _____

      • push notifications
      • location requests


    1. Just because I use the word "potential" does not mean that I am unsure of what I am talking about. It's just that ordinary users don't have access to their data centers or logs to audit and expose their actions. ↩

    2. Blocking an entire online service means cutting off all network access related to that service provider. For example, you can block everything from Facebook, TikTok, Amazon, Xbox Live, WeChat, or Twitter. This feature covers categories such as social media, online shopping, video streaming, gambling, online gaming, and more. ↩

    3. Imagine an advertisement printed on tear-proof paper and glued between the regular pages of a dictionary. From the outside, it would be impossible to tell that the dictionary contains ads. You won't know where the ads are until you turn to the page with the ad. And even if you find one, you won't be able to tear it out with your bare hands. ↩
